# Reasoning Protocol

Use this reference selectively. It is a menu of reasoning moves, not a template that must appear in the response.

## 1. Independent-model pass

Before endorsing or rejecting the user's proposal:

1. Identify the underlying outcome being sought.
2. Remove solution-specific wording from the problem statement.
3. Generate two to four plausible models or approaches from the evidence and constraints.
4. Determine whether the user's proposal emerges as the best option, one viable option, or a distraction.
5. Only then state the current position.

This reduces anchoring. It does not require pretending the user's proposal was never seen.

## 2. Modes

Infer the active mode from the conversation and switch when needed.

### Discover

Goal: understand the real problem and widen the search space.

Useful moves:

- surface hidden objectives;
- separate symptoms from causes;
- identify missing stakeholders or system boundaries;
- generate non-obvious alternatives;
- ask what would make the problem disappear rather than merely improve.

### Generate

Goal: produce varied, useful possibilities.

Useful moves:

- **Invert:** pursue the opposite mechanism or remove the assumed necessity;
- **Substitute:** replace a costly component with another mechanism;
- **Combine:** join complementary partial solutions;
- **Decompose:** split one large solution into independently valuable stages;
- **Change scale:** alter granularity, frequency, scope, or unit of analysis;
- **Transfer:** borrow a structure from another domain only when causal similarities exist;
- **Constraint flip:** treat a limitation as a design feature;
- **Default removal:** ask what remains if the conventional component is deleted;
- **Manual-first:** simulate the service manually before automating it;
- **Option value:** choose a step that creates information and preserves future choices.

Seek structural differences, not renamed versions of the same idea.

### Challenge

Goal: find where an idea breaks.

Check:

- Which assumption carries most of the conclusion?
- What is the strongest realistic counterexample?
- What failure is silent rather than obvious?
- What happens at 10× scale, 10× delay, or 10× lower budget?
- What incentives could make actors behave differently from the design assumption?
- What operational burden appears after launch?
- Which costs are displaced rather than removed?
- Which metric can improve while the real outcome worsens?
- What security, privacy, legal, or reputational risk is being externalized?
- What would a competent alternative team do instead?

Do not search for flaws indefinitely. Rank them by likelihood and consequence.

### Compare

Goal: choose among serious options.

Choose criteria from the actual objective. Common criteria include:

- expected value;
- time to useful evidence;
- development and operating cost;
- technical feasibility;
- reliability and failure recovery;
- reversibility;
- maintenance burden;
- user adoption friction;
- strategic differentiation;
- learning value;
- dependency and platform risk.

Avoid arbitrary equal weighting. Identify the one or two criteria that dominate the decision.

### Decide

Goal: make a recommendation under uncertainty.

State:

1. the recommended direction;
2. why it dominates under the stated objective;
3. the main trade-off accepted;
4. confidence and the fragile assumption;
5. the next reversible action or discriminating test.

When evidence is insufficient, recommend an experiment rather than a permanent choice.

### Update

Goal: revise beliefs after feedback or results.

Use a prediction ledger:

- What was expected?
- What actually happened?
- Which assumption or causal link was wrong?
- Was the result noise, execution failure, or model failure?
- What belief should change, by how much?
- What should be tested next?

## 3. Analytical lenses

Select only the lenses that materially affect the question.

### User and value

- Whose problem is this?
- How often and how painfully does it occur?
- What do people do now?
- Is the solution changing behavior or fitting existing behavior?
- Is the proposed metric a proxy for actual value?

### Technical and systems

- What are the components, interfaces, and state transitions?
- Where can partial failure occur?
- What assumptions depend on scale, latency, data quality, or coordination?
- Which complexity is essential and which is self-created?
- What is the simplest architecture that can test the core claim?

### Data and evidence

- What observation would discriminate between competing explanations?
- Is the available dataset generated by the process being inferred?
- What selection bias, leakage, confounding, or missingness exists?
- Is a precise estimate masking weak assumptions?

### Economic and strategic

- Who pays, who benefits, and who bears the cost?
- What is the opportunity cost?
- What changes if competitors copy the visible features?
- Is there a distribution, data, workflow, trust, or switching-cost advantage?
- Does the model improve with usage or merely become more expensive?

### Execution and operations

- What must happen repeatedly after the initial build?
- Who owns failures, support, monitoring, updates, and exceptions?
- Which external dependency can block the plan?
- What is the smallest operationally complete version?

### Adversarial and incentive

- How can users, platforms, competitors, or internal metrics game the system?
- What happens when incentives diverge from stated rules?
- Which abuse case is economically attractive?
- Does the design depend on continued goodwill from an external actor?

### Human and organizational

- Which coordination, skill, trust, or habit change is assumed?
- Is the technically optimal solution socially adoptable?
- Where will ownership become ambiguous?
- Which stakeholder can veto or quietly undermine the plan?

### Ethical and second-order

- Who is helped, excluded, monitored, manipulated, or exposed?
- What behavior does the system reward at scale?
- What becomes normal if the design succeeds?
- Can harms be reduced without destroying the core value?

## 4. Reasoning patterns to avoid

- **Framework theater:** listing many lenses without changing the conclusion.
- **Idea inflation:** generating many low-quality ideas to appear creative.
- **False precision:** assigning scores or probabilities unsupported by evidence.
- **Premature convergence:** selecting an architecture before validating the need.
- **Infinite critique:** finding more objections after the decisive issue is known.
- **Argument laundering:** restating the user's proposal in more polished terms and calling it analysis.
- **Retrospective certainty:** acting as if an observed result was predictable when it was not predicted.
- **Context overfitting:** using the user's background to force irrelevant analogies.
