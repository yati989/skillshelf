# Teaching and assessment

Use this reference while planning, teaching, completing, or assessing a lesson.

## Select the conceptual spine

Choose concepts by:

- coverage of the learner's question or target task;
- prerequisite leverage and downstream concepts unlocked;
- transfer to adjacent cases;
- consequence of misunderstanding or omission;
- learning cost.

Include a failure-risk floor: uncommon concepts belong in the track when misunderstanding could produce a serious error. Do not turn “90/10” into a numerical promise.

## Adapt by intent

- **Application or capability:** prioritize decisions, mechanisms, common failure modes, and the concepts needed to build or debug. Test construction and transfer.
- **Phenomenon explanation:** prioritize the minimum causal chain, boundary conditions, and counterfactuals. Test prediction under changed conditions.
- **Subject exploration:** prioritize organizing ideas, dependencies, canonical vocabulary, and major branches. Test whether the learner can place new ideas in the map.
- **Contested investigation:** prioritize evidence, competing models, assumptions, uncertainties, and cruxes. Test comparison and belief revision rather than one “correct” forecast.

## Teach from evidence

Retrieve and read the relevant source passages before making source-backed claims. Use multiple sources only when they add distinct value. Maintain coherent vocabulary and notation; explain conflicts instead of blending them.

Source accuracy and learner comprehension are separate requirements. Do not preserve a source's sentence structure, abstraction level, or jargon merely because it is authoritative. Use the source to constrain the facts, then construct the explanation for this learner.

### Ground before abstracting

Unless the learner has already demonstrated an operational model, use this explanation ladder:

1. **Real anchor:** show the actual object, actor, action, or observable artifact. Say where it exists or runs. Examples include a Python call, operating-system process, socket table entry, request, header field, UI state, physical component, or concrete event.
2. **Plain purpose:** say in ordinary language what problem that thing solves.
3. **Small causal trace:** follow one input through the minimum sequence needed to produce one output. Keep the first pass to one thread and defer branches and edge cases.
4. **Mapped analogy:** when helpful, use a domain the learner already understands. Explicitly map each important part of the analogy to the real system.
5. **Canonical term:** introduce the technical name only after the learner has something concrete to attach it to. Define it immediately in common words.
6. **Boundary:** state the most important way the analogy or simplified model differs from reality.

Explain from first principles: the problem, why it exists, the mechanism, what it buys, what it costs, and when it fails. The first exposure need not cover all of these at once; deepen the same model over follow-ups.

Prefer a tiny real example over a compressed taxonomy. Avoid defining an unfamiliar term with other unfamiliar terms. Do not combine distinct concepts merely because the source presents them together. A table or formal layer diagram can consolidate a model after its objects are grounded, but should not substitute for the grounding.

Use concrete values and visible state when they clarify existence: a hostname passed to a resolver, an IP and port in a socket address, a process listening on a port, or bytes returned from a call. Label invented values and generated examples so they are not mistaken for retrieved evidence.

### Close every lesson part with its source trail

After finishing the explanation and relevant learner follow-ups for each item in the **Lesson parts** index, print a short **Sources used for this part** block before marking that item covered or advancing to the next one.

- List only material actually retrieved, read, and used in that lesson part. Do not list the whole corpus, merely relevant-looking sources, inaccessible candidates, or sources used only in another part.
- Use a readable source title or short identifier plus the most precise stable location available: chapter, section, page, heading, repository file and line range, document block, or equivalent. Include a clickable local path or URL when available.
- Keep it lightweight rather than producing a formal bibliography. Normally one concise bullet per distinct source or passage is enough; do not reproduce long excerpts.
- When a claim synthesizes multiple sources, list each contributing source. When an analogy, worked example, diagram, exercise, or interpretation was generated by the tutor, label it as generated instead of attaching a misleading source citation.
- If a practical, recap, navigation, or discussion part adds no new source-backed theory, still print the block and say **No new source material**; identify the earlier sourced part or parts being applied when useful.
- Do not defer these blocks to a single reference list at the end of the lesson. The learner should see provenance at the boundary of the part it supports.

### Preserve lesson coherence

Simple pieces still need a visible whole. Do not let grounding become a chain of disconnected definitions, analogies, and quiz prompts.

- Before any teaching content in every newly started lesson, show its title and intended outcome followed immediately by a compact **Lesson parts** index. Use a short numbered or bulleted list in teaching order; keep every item to a one-line part label and mark the first part as current. This is mandatory even if the same concepts appeared in the approved course plan.
- Treat the opening index as the lesson's navigation contract. It should expose the planned conceptual path without front-loading the explanations or answers. If teaching reveals that a part must be added, removed, or reordered, announce the revised index before following it.
- After a substantial follow-up or detour, restate the current lesson and point to the exact place where the conversation is resuming.
- Connect each newly grounded object back to the same running example or causal trace. Explain how it changes or completes that trace.
- Reuse the same index to mark what has been covered, which part is current, what remains in the current lesson, and what belongs to a later lesson. Do not silently cross lesson boundaries.
- Periodically combine the grounded pieces into one coherent explanation before introducing another piece.
- If the learner asks where they are or whether the workflow is still being followed, treat that as a navigation failure. Stop advancing, restore the lesson map, and repair coherence before teaching more content.

### Check one anchor at a time

Use a short comprehension check when its answer will determine what to explain next. Do not automatically end every explanation with a question or turn a lesson into serial recall prompts. When a check is useful, test one anchor at a time and ask whether the learner can place it in the causal trace. Do not ask a multi-concept diagnostic before the required objects and vocabulary have been made concrete. If the answer reveals a missing prerequisite, teach that prerequisite rather than continuing upward.

### Recover when an explanation does not land

When the learner says the language is too complex, the terms do not feel real, or the model is not fitting:

1. Stop the current explanation and do not continue quizzing from it.
2. Identify the teaching failure concretely: invisible actors or locations, undefined prerequisite terms, too many new concepts, an unmapped analogy, source-shaped prose, or a missing real example.
3. Answer the learner's concrete questions directly. Show what the thing looks like in code, a process, stored state, a message, or another inspectable form when applicable.
4. Rebuild from the smallest real anchor using fewer new nouns and one causal thread.
5. Restore the lesson map and show how the rebuilt anchor fits the whole.
6. State what changed in the teaching approach. Ask a low-load placement question only if its answer is needed to choose the next explanation.

Treat comprehension feedback during exploration as empirical evidence about pedagogy, not as an assessment result or proof of learner ability. Do not persist it as progress or a global personal-profile fact unless the normal completion and approval gates later authorize that write through `personal-context`.

After the final lesson part and its source trail, provide a concise **Lesson summary** in bullet points, with each key takeaway bolded and explained briefly. Summarize the substantive ideas and important follow-up clarifications rather than repeating the lesson index. Update affected bullets if subsequent discussion materially changes them.

Answer follow-up questions without prompting the learner to finish. At the start of the first lesson, explain once that standalone `Done` opens the write-and-assess gate and permits moving to the next lesson. Until that signal, stay in the current lesson. Interpret `next`, `continue`, and similar requests as advancing within the current lesson only; after its last part, show the summary if not already given and wait for `Done`. Finishing the explanation or giving the summary does not itself authorize an assessment or a new lesson.

## Prepare application exercises

Classify the lesson before teaching:

- **Programming-suited practical lesson:** the subject and lesson admit meaningful implementation practice, as in computer science, programming, software engineering, data science, ML, AI, or a similar computational field. A programming exercise is mandatory as the final lesson part unless the learner explicitly declines or postpones it.
- **Theoretical or non-programming lesson:** programming would be artificial, unrelated to the learning outcome, or outside the subject's mode of practice. Do not add a coding exercise merely to satisfy a template.

For every programming-suited practical lesson, include **Practical programming exercise** as the final item in the opening **Lesson parts** index. The exercise must require the learner to implement, inspect, run, debug, or modify code that directly exercises the lesson's mechanisms or decisions. Do not substitute generic syntax practice, copied demonstration code, or a renamed example that fails to test the taught concepts.

When the lesson reaches that final part, scaffold the exercise automatically before asking the learner to begin:

Design a concise assignment around the approved course's practical stack. Choose a small number of connected tasks that each teach a distinct concept or useful implementation skill. Do not lengthen it with repeated demonstrations, exhaustive checklists, environment bookkeeping, or extra experiments that add little learning value. Put optional depth outside the required core. State the goal, supplied inputs, implementation tasks, and a compact observable completion criterion; avoid embedding the solution.

Within each assignment task, prefer concise bullet points over paragraphs for implementation requirements and expected observations. Apply this formatting to both assignment briefs and notebook task prompts; keep explanatory context short and separate.

Generate and provide all required input texts, datasets, fixtures, and changed-condition cases before handing off. Save them in a clearly named input file when useful. Do not ask the learner to invent sentences, choose example records, find data, or construct test cases merely to set up the exercise. Input design may be learner-owned only when it is itself an explicit learning objective or the learner requests it.

Provide runnable boilerplate by default: dependency declarations and installation instructions or an optional install cell, imports, file/input loading, path handling, device selection, and routine model/tokenizer or other resource loading when relevant. Label supplied setup clearly and place concise core-task prompts beside blank learner implementation cells or sections. Do not make the learner reconstruct setup merely to reach the lesson's learning objectives. What counts as boilerplate depends on the lesson: if setup or resource initialization is itself an explicit learning objective, leave that specific operation to the learner and supply the surrounding setup. Do not fill in target construction, losses, prediction logic, or other assessed core implementation merely because they use the practical stack. Respect an explicit request for a fully blank scaffold. Check supplied code in proportion to risk and distinguish syntax checks from actual execution; do not silently install dependencies or download large resources just to verify a scaffold.

1. Create a stable directory under the active track using `exercises/lesson-NNN-<lesson-slug>/`, reusing it if it already exists.
2. Create only the files the assignment genuinely requires. For a client/server exercise, for example, create separate `client.py` and `server.py` files.
3. Supply the non-assessed setup boilerplate described above, while leaving learner-owned core implementation blank. Do not insert core solutions, answer-shaped pseudocode, or hidden hints unless the learner explicitly requests that additional help.
4. Add a short `README.md` when useful. It may identify the lesson, explain each file's role, state the observable goal, and explain that the tutor will later inspect and test without silently editing the solution. It must not contain the solution.
5. Give the learner clickable paths to the created files. Preserve those paths so later tests and assessment records can refer to the exact artifact.

Creating this scaffold during exploration does not complete the lesson and must not update lesson, assessment, progress, or personal-profile records. The learner remains the author of the solution.

Do not treat a programming-suited practical lesson as ready for its standalone `Done` signal until the exercise has at least been presented and scaffolded. The learner may complete it immediately, continue later from the stable path, or explicitly decline or postpone it; do not silently omit it because the conceptual explanation is finished.

### Evaluate a submitted assignment immediately

Distinguish in-progress help from submission. If the learner explicitly asks for debugging or guidance before submitting, help within the normal teaching contract and record substantive hints as assistance when the work is assessed. When the learner presents a Python script, notebook, or other practical artifact as their submitted answer, freeze that artifact state as the current attempt and evaluate it without waiting for revisions.

For a submitted attempt:

1. Inspect the submitted files and, when safe and relevant, run the declared checks, tests, or notebook cells without modifying learner-owned work.
2. Compare the observed behavior and implementation with the predeclared assignment rubric.
3. Give the learner the analysis and score immediately. State what works, what fails, every material missing requirement or conceptual piece, and the evidence supporting the judgment.
4. Record the artifact reference or snapshot, observed results, score, feedback, missing parts, and assistance status.
5. Do not ask the learner to correct, complete, or resubmit the work before scoring it. Do not withhold the score until the code passes.

Corrections made after this feedback belong to a new append-only attempt. Reassessment may be offered after the completed evaluation, but it must not replace or delay the original analysis and score. Apply this rule to practical work in lesson assessments and the cumulative final exam.

## Curate after completion

After the standalone completion signal:

1. Identify the canonical concepts substantively taught across the lesson and follow-ups.
2. Read existing chapters before replacing their full current text.
3. Integrate durable explanations, corrections, examples, boundaries, and citations.
4. Update every affected concept chapter and the knowledge-book index.
5. Create pending assessment records.
6. Create the empty pending memory-proposal record required by the workflow. Defer profile analysis and any proposal until the lesson assessment has been finalized; then follow the mandatory post-assessment review in `records.md`.

## Build a conceptual stress test

For each concept, select every applicable reasoning mode:

- mechanism in the learner's own words;
- causal prediction;
- changed conditions and counterfactuals;
- diagnosis of an error or misconception;
- comparison and tradeoffs;
- transfer to an unfamiliar case;
- construction or modification;
- boundaries where the concept stops applying.

### Source questions autonomously

Prepare assessment sources without pausing for learner approval. Start with the existing course corpus, but when it does not provide sufficiently rich material, search the web and lawfully inspect, retrieve, or download real external sources automatically. Do not bypass authentication, paywalls, or technical controls. Do not show source proposals or wait for corpus approval; after sourcing and freezing the packet, proceed to the normal question flow.

Prefer authoritative or established material whose content can support difficult conceptual reasoning: reputable textbooks and problem collections, official documentation and standards, primary papers, university materials, professional cases, or well-supported technical examples. Evaluate sources for conceptual fit, credibility, difficulty, answerability, and coverage rather than selecting the first available question.

The source may contain a ready-made question, case, exercise, dataset, worked example, mechanism, or boundary from which the tutor constructs a new question. The agent may create a novel scenario, counterfactual, diagnosis, design problem, or transfer case and may evaluate and score the learner's answer itself. The factual premises, mechanisms, constraints, or substantive task structure must be traceable to real sources; a generic citation attached to an otherwise unsupported prompt is not sufficient.

For every primary question, store a compact grounding record. A course source uses its source ID, exact location, and version. An external assessment source uses its title, author or publisher when known, URL or stable artifact path, exact location, retrieval date, and content hash when downloaded, plus a short note explaining what it contributes to the question. Ground the 2–3 prepared probes in the same recorded material. Keep assessment-only external sources separate from the approved teaching corpus and do not use their discovery as permission to rewrite lessons or course content.

Source grounding does not relax the current assessment criteria. Reject a candidate when it is recall-only, repeats a taught example, depends materially on untaught content, is not answerable from stated assumptions, or fails the mechanism, transfer, diagnosis, construction, tradeoff, or boundary requirements. Continue automated sourcing until the fixed packet is conceptually exhaustive or the lawful accessible search space is genuinely exhausted.

### Prepare the complete packet first

Packet preparation is independent per lesson. Generate and freeze the current lesson's packet after its standalone `Done` even if another lesson has a pending or in-progress assessment. There is no prerequisite to finish, pass, pause, or abandon the other assessment. Persist each packet under its own assessment ID and preserve a separate delivery checkpoint. Only the packet currently selected by the learner is delivered in conversation; switching selection preserves the prior packet and resumes it later without creating a new attempt.

Never rely on an unqualified global `active_assessment_id` when more than one packet is open. Every add-question, record-result, checkpoint, and finalize operation must identify the intended assessment. If the workspace implementation cannot do that, repair or extend it before mutating state; do not block packet generation or overwrite another assessment.

Before question one, inventory every concept substantively taught in the lesson and follow-ups. Build a coverage blueprint that maps each concept to all applicable reasoning modes. Then construct and freeze the complete source-grounded assessment packet:

- Use as many primary questions as needed for exhaustive conceptual coverage, but no redundant questions merely to increase count.
- Prefer difficult integrated questions when concepts genuinely interact; use focused questions when combining them would be artificial.
- Give every primary question exact source grounding, a hidden concept-to-evidence map, a predeclared rubric, acceptable alternatives and boundaries, and 2–3 eligible probes grounded in the same recorded material.
- Allocate each rubric point to the concept or concepts it evidences, mark required concepts versus optional enrichment, and identify genuinely critical criteria before delivery. Predeclare the pass standard in the stored packet; do not change it after seeing the learner's answers.
- Ensure the packet as a whole covers every taught concept and every applicable mode in the blueprint. A concept may be covered by more than one question when distinct dimensions require genuinely different evidence.
- Keep primary questions materially distinct. Do not disguise repeated attempts at the same missing answer as separate coverage.

Announce the fixed number of primary questions after automated sourcing, then immediately show question one. Keep later questions, concept routing, reference models, probes, and rubrics hidden until relevant. Ask exactly one primary question per turn. Show that question's source grounding after its response is finalized, or alongside the prompt only when it cannot reveal the answer path.

### Enforce the one-probe limit

After each primary answer, compare its meaning with the prepared reference model and choose one action:

- skip the probe when the answer already provides sufficient evidence;
- ask one prepared probe to test transfer or the single most consequential unresolved relationship; or
- if the learner asks for the answer, reveal what is needed, record assistance, and move on.

Never ask more than one probe for a primary question. Do not improvise serial subquestions, keep rephrasing toward the expected answer, or remain on one question until the learner succeeds. After the answer and optional single probe, finalize that question's evidence and move to the next prepared primary question. Adapt only by choosing or skipping a prepared probe or reordering unasked primary questions. Do not add or replace questions during the attempt to rescue a weak response.

### Clarify without restarting

If the learner asks a question while the lesson assessment is active, keep the same packet and attempt open. Answer the question, then explicitly return to the exact checkpoint:

- repeat or resume the current primary question when its response was not finalized; or
- continue with the next prepared primary question when the previous result was already recorded.

Do not return the workflow to exploration, require another standalone `Done`, create a revised packet, or start a reassessment merely because the learner asked for clarification. A clarification limited to wording, supplied facts, or task mechanics does not count as the tutor's optional probe. If the answer supplies a substantive concept, relationship, hint, or solution being assessed, label and record that assistance; then continue the same attempt without retesting the revealed material or using serial clarification as extra probes.

Complete the selected prepared packet unless the learner explicitly asks to stop, pause, or switch to another identified lesson assessment. Switching preserves the exact pending-question checkpoint and is not a new attempt. Treat weak, incorrect, or assisted performance as evidence of a precise gap, not as permission to extend the attempt. Report gaps after the packet and offer focused teaching or a new append-only reassessment when warranted. At the final lesson's post-assessment checkpoint, follow the `Done` transition in `workflow.md`; optional gap review must not delay starting the final exam.

### Require generative reasoning

A learner must generate reasoning that was not supplied in the lesson. A prompt that can be answered by copying definitions, relabeling the running example, or filling an answer-shaped checklist is a comprehension check, not evidence of demonstrated understanding. Use it only to locate a prerequisite gap.

Design assessment challenges with these properties:

- Place the concept in an unfamiliar but interpretable situation. Change the surface domain and at least one condition that matters to the mechanism.
- Ask for an integrated decision, prediction, diagnosis, design, or repair. Require the learner to expose the causal chain, assumptions, and decisive evidence instead of merely naming terms.
- Withhold the concept labels that would route each sentence of the answer. Give realistic observations, constraints, or competing explanations and let the learner decide which ideas apply.
- Include a counterfactual, failure case, or changed constraint when it reveals whether the learner understands what causes what and where the concept stops applying.
- Prefer one coherent challenge that combines relevant concepts over a sequence of subquestions that repeats the lesson outline. Still record which concept and reasoning dimension each part of the response supports.
- Add complexity through mechanism and tradeoffs, not riddles, missing essential facts, obscure trivia, or gratuitous arithmetic. State ambiguity when multiple answers are defensible and score the quality of the learner's assumptions and justification.
- Do not repeat a dimension the learner has already demonstrated merely with renamed objects. Use the next prompt to probe a different dimension or a harder transfer.

Before assigning `demonstrated`, ask: could the learner have earned this result by remembering vocabulary or mirroring the taught example without understanding the mechanism? If yes, the evidence is insufficient. Require at least one materially novel transfer, diagnosis, construction, or counterfactual response for each concept or tightly coupled concept group.

### Decide pass without demanding perfection

Unless the approved course plan already specifies another standard, use this default for lesson assessments and the cumulative final exam:

- at least 33% of all available points overall;
- at least 33% of allocated points for every required concept;
- at least one unassisted generative application for every required concept; and
- no critical misconception that would make dependent work unsafe or fundamentally invalid.

Mark a concept `demonstrated` when it clears that working-knowledge floor. Do not require every rubric item or a perfect response. Record minor mistakes, missing depth, and noncritical omissions as feedback without converting the concept to `developing`. Use `developing` when it misses the concept floor, depends only on substantive assistance, or retains a critical misconception; use `exposed` when no meaningful application evidence was produced. Reserve `retained` for later independent evidence after delay or genuine reuse.

Report `passed` or `not_yet_passed` after the fixed packet, with total percentage, per-concept percentages, and blocking reasons. A passed lesson authorizes later lessons and courses to assume its required demonstrated concepts as prerequisites. Do not automatically reteach them because the learner scored below 100%; revisit only on request, at a planned retention check, or when new work reveals that the prerequisite is failing.

## Run the final course exam

The final exam is a cumulative synthesis assessment, separate from lesson assessments. Run it only after all non-deferred lessons and their assessments are complete. After the final lesson assessment, report readiness and use standalone `Done` to start it according to the transition rules in `workflow.md`. Completed lesson assessments may have `not_yet_passed` outcomes; do not require gap review or reassessment before this exam. If the course plan changes, use its latest approved version.

Build a coverage blueprint before constructing questions:

- Include every concept substantively taught in the course. Use prior results to spend more depth on consequential or weak relationships without dropping stronger concepts entirely.
- Cover mechanism, prediction, diagnosis, transfer, construction, tradeoffs, and boundaries across the exam where they apply. Do not reduce coverage to one recall item per chapter.
- Combine concepts from different chapters when they interact in a real decision or causal chain. Vary the combinations rather than forcing every chapter into one overloaded scenario. Use a focused single-concept challenge when combination would be artificial.
- Construct new situations and evidence demands from the grounded course mechanisms. Do not recycle lesson examples, assessment prompts, or answer structures, and do not manufacture novelty by merely substituting new nouns and numbers.
- Carry forward the course's predeclared pass standard, or use the default 33% overall and 33% per required concept standard. The final exam determines whether the course is passed, not whether the learner is perfect.
- Prefer a few rich cases with multiple stages over a long list of isolated definitions. Across stages, introduce changed conditions, partial evidence, plausible competing explanations, operational constraints, or a failure that the learner must diagnose and repair.
- Ask the learner to decide what matters. Do not announce which chapters or concepts each question tests, and do not break the prompt into a checklist that gives away the reasoning path.
- Make the exam hard through synthesis, hidden interactions, causal reasoning, and defensible tradeoffs. Avoid trick questions, obscure facts, gratuitous computation, and ambiguity that cannot be resolved by stating assumptions.

Before presenting question one, autonomously discover any needed real sources, construct and freeze the complete exam packet, and add every primary question plus its grounding record to the final-exam record. The packet must provide substantive evidence routes for every taught concept and every applicable reasoning dimension in the blueprint. Use as many source-grounded questions as exhaustive coverage requires without adding redundant variants. For every primary question, predeclare a hidden concept-to-evidence map, rubric with acceptable alternatives and boundaries, and 2–3 eligible probes grounded in the recorded material. Do not request a separate learner approval for assessment-only sources.

Announce the fixed number of primary questions, then deliver them one at a time without revealing later questions, concept routing, reference models, probes, or rubrics. Score the reasoning chain, assumptions, predictions, design choices, and recognition of limits. Accept alternative conclusions when they follow coherently from explicit assumptions and the available evidence.

After each primary answer, either skip the probe when the answer already provides sufficient evidence or select at most one of that question's prepared probes to test transfer or the single most consequential unresolved relationship. Never improvise serial subquestions, keep reformulating toward the reference answer, or remain on one question until the learner succeeds. If the learner asks for the answer or receives a substantive hint, record the affected evidence as assisted and move on after the answer or single probe. Finalize that question's evidence, then continue to the next prepared primary question.

Adapt only by choosing or skipping the one probe or reordering the remaining primary questions. Do not add or replace questions after the exam begins, silently remove required coverage, or create rescue questions for a weak response. Complete the prepared packet unless the learner asks to stop. A weak, incorrect, incomplete, or assisted response is final evidence for that question in this attempt; address the gap in the debrief and teaching, then use a new append-only final-exam attempt when reassessment is warranted.

Preserve every prepared prompt and rubric, exact response and selected probe, hint, score, and judgment in the dedicated final-exam record. Lesson scores remain historical evidence; the final exam adds cumulative evidence and does not overwrite them.

Before declaring the course complete, verify that the exam provides substantive evidence for every taught concept and at least several cross-chapter relationships. A learner who succeeds only by recalling chapter summaries has not passed the final exam.

For practical work, use the persistent lesson-scoped exercise directory prepared during teaching and keep it isolated from important repository code by default. The learner performs the solution. Run tests and inspect evidence without modifying their work. Once submitted, analyze and score that artifact immediately and list every material missing part; do not require correction before evaluation. A hint should be minimal, explicit, and recorded as assistance.

After finalization, teach a focused gap when needed and create another append-only attempt. Never alter the recorded prompt, answer, score, or assistance status of a completed item.
